I am an orphan.

Nov. 28 - Maria watched him eat before asking, "Your name." Then, more softly, "Vittorio." He knew what she meant. Vittorio was all he had—no father’s name, no family to leave him one. "Just, 'Vittorio'?" The question was innocent, but he lowered his eyes and, after a long silence, traced the answer into her palm. "Just... Vittorio." "...Oh." Maria came down beside him and touched his cheek.

"I'm... I'm sorry. I was..." Vittorio shook his head before she could finish and pressed his face briefly into her hand. At last he forced himself to meet her eyes. "I have... no family name." He paused, then added the harder truth. "I am an orphan." He rested his forehead against her hand, unable to look at her. Vittorio could not know that, only moments earlier, Maria had been thinking of the name “Maria Park.” Had he known, he might have gone straight to City Hall and demanded a surname of his own.

[We need to move. Now. North, then west. There's a train line past the river. We can lose them in the mountains.] "Let's go, then. I'll do whatever you tell me to do." Maria’s voice was low and calm, but the resolve within it was harder than the winter air filling the cabin. Vittorio stopped the pen moving across the map and slowly raised his head. Her eyes were narrowed, though not from distrust or suspicion. It was the look of someone desperately gathering herself before the single urgent purpose of survival. She would do whatever he told her. The words were both a shackle and a salvation. The life resting upon his shoulders had become too heavy to measure, yet at the same time it gave absolute purpose to everything he did.

Vittorio nodded once without a word. His violet eyes darkened. This was no time for sentiment. Her trust had to be justified through action. He rose and picked up the black coat that had been tossed carelessly across the bed. It was the same coat he had covered her with the night before, still faintly carrying her warmth and scent. He placed it around Maria’s shoulders. The heavy wool looked absurd over her ivory silk dress, but they had long since lost the luxury of caring whether anything looked proper.

On January 14, they moved again. A single stone from the sapphire necklace was all they had left. Vittorio sewed it deep into the lining of his trousers and waited until Maria’s feet had healed enough to travel. They disembarked at Albany, took a bus, then walked again. West. West, and then north.They abandoned names, erased tracks and changed clothes. Even after the neon of New York disappeared beyond the horizon, Vittorio continued to glance behind them every few days. Nothing followed except their long shadows.

They reached a mountain village in Vermont in the middle of February. Snow stood waist-deep in places, and the town held perhaps three hundred people. There was one post office, one general store, one church, and at the edge of the village, backed against the woods, one shabby wooden house. Vittorio sold the final sapphire fragment and paid six months’ rent. The jewels Giancarlo had once placed around Maria’s neck had, in the end, purchased Maria’s survival. Vittorio found work at a sawmill outside the village. Word soon spread that the enormous new laborer could not speak, but his size and silent diligence pleased the mill owner. Maria began teaching violin to children at the village church.
